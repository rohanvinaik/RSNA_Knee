Each and EVERY door/goat/car/choice is a unique, typed object in multi-dimensional space, encodable through balanced ternary identity. The three doors are (1) the one you chose, (2) not-chosen "goat", and (3) not-chosen "?". After door (2) is revealed, assuming you're not hungry for mutton, the "car" door is a conditional assignment of identity to one of the two remaining doors. But here is where it gets *fun*. But basically, much like there are different "infinities" (e.g. the infinite number of numbers between 0 and 1, and "infinity" as an unbounded upper limit to numbers themselves), "zero" is not a single element. The monty hall problem. You have doors (1) and (3) left. Naive probability dictates they're the same. But there's an *orthogonal* channel of information that naive probability isn't accounting for--these doors are NOT identical. (1) is the door you picked, (3) is the one you didn't. In that COMPLETELY orthogonal informational channel, "your pick" has a 1/3 chance of having a car, "not your pick" has a 2/3 chance. Thus, the knowability matrix of \<your pick or no, goat or car> is <1,0> for door (1), and <-1,0> for door (3). And P(car) is 1/3 for <1,?> and 2/3 for <-1,0>. Note the elegance there. Not only do assignment of identity as balanced ternary allow for mathematical encoding of semantic objects, but they also *inherently* encode the mathematics of the problem as well, for the sum of probabilities there *necessarily* must add to 1
