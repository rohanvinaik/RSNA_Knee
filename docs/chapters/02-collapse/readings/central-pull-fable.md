# Second reading — the coastline pull and the central pull, for bone

I read §3 and §4 as given, and the five rows of the figure. Where I state something as a consequence, it follows from the stated source behaviour; where I could not determine it from what I was given, I say so.

## Two things I can derive before the questions, because they change the answers

**A. Dark places are unlabelled, so a `peer_territory` edge is not "in the middle of a dark cortical gap".**
In every right panel the dark pixels are black, not coloured: the speckle inside the muscle territories of rows 1 and 4, the black seam running along the femoral condyle between 3 and 4 in row 3, the black crescent at the bottom of row 5. So whatever `interior` is, it excludes `dark` at the split level — consistent with `mask=interior` being (or being a subset of) `available & ~dark`. Then a `peer_territory` edge is, by definition of the watershed with `connectivity=1`, a place where two *non-dark* interior pixels are 4‑adjacent across the divide. The lead's §5(3) says such a divide sits "in the middle of a dark cortical gap, on neither bone's surface". That cannot be the case at the edge pixel itself: there is no dark there. A peer divide exists only where the interior is *connected* across — either through continuous tissue (row 2: femur 3 ↔ tibia 6 across the joint line; the 3|6 jagged boundary; row 1: the straight cut across the femoral shaft between 5 and 7; row 2: territory 3 wrapping down the lateral soft tissue to y≈850, well outside the condyle) or through a bright bridge in an otherwise dark line. The "dark gap with a divide in the middle" case can only arise if `interior` is a *filled* mask that includes dark pixels while `distance` was computed on `available & ~dark`; then every dark gap is a zero‑plateau of `−distance` and the flood meets at the plateau's midline. **I cannot tell from §4 which `interior` is passed to `watershed`.** The picture says "excludes dark"; the lead's reading says "includes dark". This has to be settled before question 2 means anything, so: *what exactly is `interior`?*

**B. On a peer edge the prior is the only force, by the sibling's own rule.**
§3: an unread slope, or a slope that does not fall, returns the ceiling. Along the normal of a peer divide in continuous tissue there is no falling edge, so every such point gets `band = ceiling` — maximally free — and the chord/oval steps act on it unopposed. Conversely at a well‑measured cortical edge the band is small and the prior is nearly inert. So the question "oval or axis prior?" is, in practice, the question "what happens to the peer divides?", because those are the only places with room to move. Whatever is chosen there will be shape‑prior fiction unless the peer edges are first re‑seated onto something measured or given a band of zero. This is not a redesign suggestion; it is where the existing rules put the consequence.

## Q1 — does the measured medial axis replace the two‑focus oval?

Partly, and the lead's version imports more than "grows radially from marrow" earns, in three identifiable ways.

1. **The ridge in the picture is not the bone's axis; it is the axis of the connected imaged interior.** `distance` is from *all* surrounding constraints, so inside a territory near a peer edge the nearest constraint is often on the other side of the divide. In row 2 the brightest ridge runs continuously from the femoral peak (325, 720) through the joint saddle into the tibial peak (330, 945); in row 3 the saddle itself got a peak (325, 1350) and became territory 4. A bone's "own ridge" exists only after the bone is isolated, which is after the outline — the prior would be built from the thing it is supposed to constrain. The oval does not have this circularity because area and second moments are integrals of the loop as traced, and the band clamp bounds how far the loop can be wrong.

2. **The axis is differentially unstable; the oval is integrally stable.** Every "star" in the left panels — the rays from (325, 720) to the corners of the orange blob in row 2, the fan under (280, 1920) in row 4 — is a medial‑axis spur generated by a boundary corner. Spurs are produced by exactly the staircase/coastline noise the pull exists to remove, and feed it back as a prior. The medial‑axis transform is lossless: axis + radius function *is* the shape, so "pull toward a smooth offset from the ridge" is equivalent to "smooth the radius function along the axis" — a perfectly deterministic operation, but one whose scale (how smooth, over what axial length) is a chosen number, not a measured one, unless it is taken from the bone's own well‑measured `low_signal` segments.

3. **Where "radial from marrow" fails as geometry.** *Patella*: a sesamoid, nearly axis‑less — the two‑focus oval is the better prior there, and in row 4 it did not even get its own territory (the anterior strip 4 runs from the quadriceps region down past where the patella should be; in row 5, territory 1 is a crescent that may be patella plus prepatellar tissue — I cannot confirm the anatomy from the figure). *Condyles*: a branch point of the axis; the radius function is non‑smooth at the intercondylar notch by nature, and a smooth‑offset prior would fill the notch exactly as a coastline pull fills a bay (rows 2–4 all show the notch as the deepest concavity of the femoral territory). *Osteophyte*: a radius spike — the finding is the violation. *Fracture*: see Q4. So the honest statement is: the axis prior is right for the diaphysis and for nothing else in the picture; the oval is right for the patella; neither is right at the notch. The founder's text supports a smoothness propensity on the surface; it does not supply a global model of what the surface is smooth *relative to*.

On reading (2): the watershed is the central *assignment*, not a central *pull*. It decides which centre a place belongs to; it moves no boundary point. The sibling's oval step is the thing that moves points. Calling the watershed "the central pull" conflates partition with outline, and the peer edges are where that conflation shows.

## Q2 — re‑seat the territory boundary onto the near end of each bracketed interval, then pull taut?

Given A and B above:

- A bracket requires a whole available profile with exactly one below‑split run flanked on both sides. On a `low_signal` edge the territory already abuts the dark run; the near end of the bracket is approximately where the edge already is, so re‑seating there is a small correction (sub‑band), and the pull is meaningful.
- On a `peer_territory` edge in continuous tissue (row 2 joint line; row 1 shaft cut; row 2's lateral soft‑tissue wrap of territory 3), **there is no dark interval on the normal to re‑seat onto**. The watershed divide there is not a wrong starting curve for an inter‑bone border; it is not an inter‑bone border at all. It is a line through one material (joint‑line case) or through one bone's marrow (shaft case). No pull and no re‑seating fixes that, because the bracketing rule returns nothing.
- On a `peer_territory` edge that is a bright bridge in a dark line, the normal profile at the bridge point also has no below‑split run (the bridge is above split by construction), so again no bracket *at that point* — the bracket exists on the neighbouring `low_signal` points on either side. Whether the sibling's chord step then carries those neighbours' positions across the bridge depends on the bridge's length versus the band; a bridge shorter than the band closes, by the "hole narrower than its band closes" test. That is the one case where the proposed re‑seat‑then‑pull works, and it works by the existing mechanism, not by anything new.

So: the territory boundary is a usable starting curve exactly on its `low_signal` and short‑bridge fraction, and unusable on the rest. The fraction is measurable now (Q5).

One more derivable point: if `interior` *does* include dark (the lead's reading), then the "near end" of a bracket is on *one* bone's surface and the far end on the other's, and re‑seating one territory's loop onto the near end while the neighbour's loop goes to its own near end produces two loops that no longer share an edge — which is correct, and means the output is no longer a partition. That is fine for outlines but it breaks the edge typing in §4, which assumes a shared boundary. Worth knowing before doing it.

## Q3 — "exponential decay of propensity for violations" as a measured per‑point allowance

Two different decays are being asked about, and only one is already in the machinery.

*Measurement allowance.* `band = flicker/slope` is a hard box: propensity 1 inside, 0 outside. Once the Rician VST is composed with the sampler so that flicker is in sigmas, `flicker/slope` becomes millimetres per sigma, and the box of width k sigmas is a measured quantity with one chosen number (k). The tail outside it is Gaussian, not exponential, and it is the tail of the *measurement*, not of the shape. Right now, with the VST not composed, the band is in raw intensity units divided by raw slope — I cannot determine what "sub‑band" means physically in the current pipeline, and neither can anyone else until that composition exists.

*Shape propensity.* The founder's sentence is about the surface, not the noise. Here the exponential decay is **already structurally present in the sibling's step (1)**, and provably so: moving each point halfway to the chord between its neighbours multiplies a boundary perturbation of wavenumber κ by cos²(κ/2) per iteration. A high‑curvature violation of amplitude a decays geometrically with iteration count at a rate that increases with curvature; a low‑curvature one barely moves. The band clamp then decides the only thing that matters — whether the violation is allowed to decay to zero or is held at amplitude ≥ a − band. So the taut loop is "the smoothest curve that stays within every point's measured band", which is a deterministic reading of "exponential decay of propensity for violations, except where evidence holds them." No fitted penalty is needed to get that behaviour; what is *not* measured is the rate (the ½), and I see nothing in §3/§4 that measures it. If a measured rate is wanted, the only self‑referential source in the current pipeline would be the curvature statistics of a bone's own `low_signal` segments — I note that as the available quantity, not as a proposal.

## Q4 — fracture versus smoothing

The sibling's waist argument does not carry, and the test suite says so directly.

- The waist survives because shortening perimeter at *fixed area* cannot pinch a neck below the band without changing area. A cortical break is not a neck. A non‑displaced fracture is not a boundary feature at all: the outer surface stays smooth and the finding is an *interior* line (dark on fat‑bright, with edema bright on fat‑suppressed). The outline pull neither sees it nor can erase it; the risk and the evidence live in the interior profile, and the two‑acquisition comparison the founder calls the gift is where it would show (present in one, absent or inverted in the other).
- A displaced fracture is a step in the surface. A step is a sharp, high‑slope edge, so by `band = flicker/slope` the points on it get the *tightest* bands in the whole loop and are the hardest to move. The step survives because it is well measured, not because of topology. That is the actual protection, and it is already in the rule.
- An incomplete fracture that reaches the surface is a notch. "A hole narrower than its band closes" and "a sub‑band wiggle comes off" are pinned by test. **A notch narrower than the local band is erased by the pull, provably.** Whether real fracture lines in these images are wider than the local band is an empirical question I cannot answer from the figure, and it cannot be answered at all until the VST is composed and the band has a physical width.

So the argument that is needed is not topological; it is "the finding has slope, slope shrinks the band, a small band is immovable" — and its failure mode is "a finding with no slope at the outline (a crack below resolution) has no protection, and should not be looked for on the outline."

## Q5 — what to measure first on this picture

In order, each computable from quantities §4 already produces:

1. **Per territory, the boundary length by edge type**, and specifically the `peer_territory` fraction. By point B, that fraction is the fraction of the loop on which the pull would act unopposed. From the figure I expect it to be near zero for row 3's femur (3) and tibia (5), substantial for row 2's femur (its entire joint‑line edge against 6 and its lateral wrap), near one for row 1's shaft territory 5 along its lower cut, and near one for the muscle territories in rows 1 and 4. If that is what the numbers say, a taut pull is meaningful for row 3's bones now and for nothing else yet.

2. **The saddle height of every peer divide**: `distance` in millimetres along the divide. A divide at several millimetres of EDT is in continuous tissue and no bracket will exist; a divide at ≈ one spacing is a bridge in a dark line and the existing hole‑closing behaviour applies. This single number sorts the peer edges into "not a boundary" and "fixable by what exists."

3. **Bracket success rate and interval width on `low_signal` edges**, and the offset between the territory edge and the near end. If the offset is within the band almost everywhere, re‑seating is a no‑op and the pull suffices; if it is routinely larger, the territory edge is systematically inside the cortex and re‑seating is doing real work.

4. **Agreement between the two acquisitions of one site.** I cannot confirm from the figure which rows are the same coronal site (the frames are cropped differently and no caption says so); if rows 2 and 3 are, then the first real test of "close enough to the true surface" is the distance, point by point, between the `low_signal` edges of territory 3 in each — measured in mm, compared to each point's band. Where both acquisitions put a `low_signal` edge within one band of each other, that is the surface; where only one does, that is the founder's gift and the point to look at; where neither does, the territory edge there is unsupported by any measurement and the pull should not be trusted.

5. **One sanity check on the markers**: row 3's territory 4 is a peak in the joint saddle and row 4's territory 4 runs from the quadriceps region down the entire anterior femur. Both are consequences of `prominence_mm`, not of the pull, and no smoothing operates at the territory level. I note them because any outline evaluation done on those territories measures the marker choice, not the pull.
